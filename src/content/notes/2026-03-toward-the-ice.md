Most of what I do ends in a document: a hazard map, a report, a dashboard.
The people who most need to understand the landscape — the ones living on
it or walking across it — rarely read those.

Expedition science interpretation closes that gap. On a polar ship or a
mountain trail the geologist's job is not to model the slope but to explain,
in ten minutes, why this fjord is shaped like that, what the moraine says
about the last century, and why the rock underfoot is older than the ocean
in front of you.

It turns out this is the same skill as making a good map: pick the one
variable that carries the story, remove everything else, and say it in a way
that survives wind.

So I am preparing — courses, field-craft, a growing set of study notes on
Antarctic and Arctic geology and ecology — and building small tools along
the way: an expedition mapper that turns a GPS track into a terrain map, a
field-guide reference for polar locations and species. This section of the
notebook will grow as that does.
