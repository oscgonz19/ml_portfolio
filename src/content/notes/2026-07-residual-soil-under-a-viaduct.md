Station 6 is not spectacular. A concrete viaduct, a grassy embankment, a
few metres of orange-brown soil with roots in it. Most people walk past it.

But that soil is the whole story of the Quindío plateau. It is the
**Armenia Formation** — volcaniclastic material that came down from the
Ruiz–Tolima massif and blanketed everything — after a few hundred thousand
years of tropical rain. What you see is not sediment any more. It is a
*residual soil*: rock that weathered in place until it forgot it was rock.

Residual soils are why slopes here behave the way they do. They hold a steep
face for years, then a single wet season finds a root channel or an old cut
and a slab lets go. In the boreholes along this corridor the pattern repeats
seven times: a thin cap of clayey sand over several metres of high-plasticity
silt, no water table to six metres, and anthropic fill wherever the railway
builders needed to level something.

The engineering answer is not complicated — drainage, gentle cuts, no
loading on the crest — but it only works if someone reads the ground first.
That is what the hammer is for.
